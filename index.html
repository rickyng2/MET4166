Song-length planner

Songs feel “too short” or “drag on” because of maths, not talent: length = bars × 4 beats × (60 ÷ BPM). Set your tempo and section sizes here first — GarageBand will simply follow this map. One bar = 4 beats in 4/4 time.

Song structure — the parts of a song

A song is not one long stream of music — it is a handful of standard parts, each with a different job. Learn these seven names and you can describe, plan and copy almost any song you hear, including the three sample songs below. The planner above already uses five of them.

One typical pop song, end to end — the planner’s Pop groove preset. Tap any block to jump to that part’s card.

Intro

4–8 bars

The opening of the song. Before anything big happens, it sets the mood, the key and the speed — usually with just one or two layers playing.

Everyday picture: the first page of a story — or walking into a room and looking around before anyone speaks.

In GarageBand: drums + chords only. Hold the bass and the sparkle back, so the verse has somewhere to grow from.

Verse

8–16 bars

The storytelling part. The melody stays lower, simpler and quieter, leaving room to build. When the verse comes back, the music is the same — in a sung song, only the words change.

Everyday picture: the chapters of a book — same world, new details each time.

In GarageBand: every layer except sparkle. Keep the melody simple and lower, like the verse melody in Step 5.

Pre-Chorus

4–8 bars · optional

A short, optional ramp between the verse and the chorus. The energy, pitch or rhythm creeps upward, so the chorus feels like an arrival instead of just the next block.

Everyday picture: the run-up before a jump — or the staircase up to the big room.

In GarageBand: steal the last 4 bars of your verse — add the shaker layer and let the melody climb. The three sample songs skip it to stay simple.

Chorus

8–16 bars

The main event: the song’s biggest message and its catchiest melody, at full energy. It returns several times, usually exactly the same each time — that repetition is the point.

Everyday picture: the headline of the song — if a friend remembers one part, it is this one.

In GarageBand: everything on — drums, extra percussion, bass, chords, sparkle, and your best melody. This is where your hook lives.

Hook

2–4 bars · inside the chorus

Not a place in the song — an ingredient. The hook is the short, catchy bit (a melody phrase, a riff, or one sung line) designed to get stuck in the listener’s head.

Everyday picture: the part you catch yourself humming in the shower the next day.

In GarageBand: your hook is the best 2–4 bars of your Launchkey improvisation. Repeat it exactly in every chorus — repetition is what turns a nice phrase into a hook.

Bridge

0–8 bars · optional

A one-time detour near the end: a different melody, sometimes different chords, or everything stripped right down. It refreshes the listener’s ears so the final chorus feels new again.

Everyday picture: a plot twist near the end of a film — a short side trip just before the finale.

In GarageBand: try chords only, or play the melody an octave lower. All three sample songs leave the bridge out — it is a spice, not a requirement.

Outro

4–8 bars

The ending — the song says goodbye. Layers drop away one by one, the sparse layout of the intro comes back, or everything simply fades out.

Everyday picture: the end credits of a film — the story is over, but the feeling lingers a moment longer.

In GarageBand: copy your intro to the end, or draw a fade-out on the Master volume over the last 2 bars (Step 7).

The composing sequence

Always build in this order: drums → bass → chords → sparkle → melody → arrange → export. Each layer locks to the one below it, so nothing ever sounds “off”. Work section by section (verse first is fine) and keep saving with ⌘S.

1

Set up the project

Goal: a blank project that already knows your tempo and key, so every loop you drag in lines up.

  • Open GarageBand → Empty Project → choose Software Instrument and click Create. (You can delete this first track later — or keep it for the melody.)
  • In the LCD display at the top, set the tempo to the BPM from your planner (double-click the number and type it), set key signature to match, and leave time signature at 4/4.
  • Turn on the metronome K and count-in ⇧K — the count-in gives you one bar of clicks before recording starts, essential when you record the melody later.
  • Save immediately: ⌘S, name it something like “First Song 001”. Save again after every finished step.
Why this order? Apple Loops automatically stretch to the project tempo and transpose to the project key — but only if you set both before you start dragging loops in.
2

Drums first — the skeleton

Goal: a drum groove running the full length of the song. Everything else is built on top of this grid.

  • Open the Loop Browser with O. Tick Drums / Percussion under instrument filters, and filter by your genre mood (e.g. “Chill”, “Pop”, “Electronic”).
  • Click any loop to preview it. Pick one you could happily listen to for two minutes — simple beats beat busy beats.
  • Drag it onto an empty area of the track lane. Then hover at the loop’s top-right corner until the curved arrow appears and drag right to repeat it — or select it and ⌥-drag (Option-drag) to copy — until it covers your whole planned length.
  • For the chorus, layer a second percussion loop (shaker or tambourine) on a new track underneath — same dragging method. Chorus = drums + extra layer, verse = drums alone. That contrast is what makes a chorus feel bigger.
Keep it in the family: loops whose names start the same (e.g. “Downtown Funk Beat”, “Downtown Funk Bass”, “Downtown Funk Chords”) are designed to fit together perfectly. Start with one family and your song is almost guaranteed to gel.
3

Bass + chords — the harmony floor

Goal: the chord progression from your planner, carried by a bass loop and a chord loop, in the same key as the drums.

  • Back in the Loop Browser O, filter Bass. Choose a loop from the same family as your drums if possible; drag it in on a new track and stretch it the full length, exactly like the drums.
  • Add a chord layer: filter Piano / Guitar / Synth Pad and pick a loop that plays chords (names often include the progression or “Chords”). Piano and soft guitar loops are the most forgiving for a first song.
  • Check the loop browser’s key column — loops in your project key (or its relative minor) always fit. A loop in C fits a C major song; an A minor loop also fits C major, because they share the same notes.
  • Press Space and listen to drums + bass + chords together. If anything clashes, swap that one loop — don’t try to fix harmony loop by loop.

The beginner-proof progression: the planner above shows the famous “four chords” for your key (I–V–vi–IV). Thousands of hit songs use only these — your loop family almost certainly does too.
4

Add one sparkle layer

Goal: one extra texture — an arpeggio, pluck or pad — used sparingly so the song feels alive, not crowded.

  • Filter loops by Synth → look for “Arpeggio”, “Pluck” or “Pad” in the name, same key/family as before.
  • Use it only in the chorus (and maybe the intro). Select the regions outside the chorus and delete them, or mute the track outside those sections with region copies.
  • Pull this track’s volume fader down so it sits behind the chords — sparkle should be felt more than heard.
Rule of thumb: maximum 4–5 tracks for a first song. If you want to add a sixth sound, something else has to leave. Crowding is the #1 beginner mistake.
5

Improvise the melody on the Launchkey

Goal: record a simple, singable melody over the chorus first, then a simpler variation for the verse.

  • Plug the Launchkey into your Mac with its USB cable — it is class-compliant, no driver needed. In GarageBand, create a Software Instrument track and pick a clear lead sound (a piano, “Classic Electric Piano”, or a soft synth lead — avoid pads for melody, they blur).
  • On the Launchkey, turn on Scale mode and set it to your song’s key: the pads light up with only the in-key notes, and the keys can’t produce a “wrong” note outside the scale. Alternatively use Chord mode to play full chords from single keys while you explore.
  • Just play along with the loop for a few minutes without recording. Melodies come from rhythm more than from notes: try short-short-loooong patterns, repeat one small phrase (a “motif”) and answer it with a slightly different one.
  • Set the cycle region (drag in the ruler) over just the 16 chorus bars, turn cycle on with C, press record R and improvise take after take — each pass is kept, and you pick the best one afterwards. No pressure to be perfect: Step 6 fixes the timing.
  • Record the verse melody the same way — usually the same motif, lower and quieter. Use the Launchkey’s Octave +/− buttons if your idea sits too high or low.

Your safety net: in C major, every white key is a good note

GarageBand loops in C major (or A minor) only use these notes — so anything you play on the white keys will fit. Start and end phrases on the highlighted root note and the melody will sound “finished”.

Stuck for an idea? Copy the rhythm of a lyric you like — tap the syllables on one single key, then move the same rhythm across 2–3 neighbouring white keys. That is genuinely how many melodies start.
6

Quantize — tidy the timing

Goal: your human, slightly wobbly performance snaps neatly onto the beat grid — without re-recording anything.

  • Double-click your recorded melody region to open the Piano Roll editor (or select the region and press E).
  • Select all the notes: click inside the editor, then ⌘A.
  • In the editor’s left panel, open Time Quantize and choose 1/16 Note for busy melodies or 1/8 Note for simple ones. Drag the Strength slider: 100% = perfectly robotic; 75–90% keeps a human feel while fixing the wobble. This is the “simplify & align” step — notes move to the nearest grid line.
  • Still in the editor, tick “Limit to Key”: any note you accidentally played outside the scale can now be dragged only onto in-key notes — drag strays up or down one step until they sit right.
  • Delete obvious accidents (click a note, Delete), and drag note ends to tidy lengths. Play it back — done.
Quantize is per-region and reversible: it never changes your original performance permanently while editing — if a passage sounds too stiff, lower the Strength or press ⌘Z and try 1/8 instead.
7

Arrange the sections

Goal: turn your full-length layers into the intro / verse / chorus map from the planner, using arrangement by subtraction.

  • Your layers currently run the whole song. Shape them: in the intro, delete (or mute) the bass and sparkle regions so only drums + chords play; in the verse, everything except sparkle; in the chorus, everything on.
  • To move a whole section: drag-select across all tracks for those bars, ⌘C, click where it should go, ⌘V. Split regions cleanly at the playhead with ⌘T; join neighbours back with ⌘J.
  • Add a tiny drum fill or crash loop (search “fill”) in the last bar before each chorus — one bar of difference is enough to make the chorus feel like an event.
  • Outro: copy the intro’s sparse layout to the end. For a gentle landing, open Automation (press A), choose Volume on the Master track, and draw a fade-out over the last 2 bars.
  • Play the whole song top to bottom and check it against the planner’s map — sections should change exactly where the map says.
Energy rule: each section should add or remove at least one layer compared with the one before. If two sections sound identical, the listener hears one long blur.
8

Quick mix, then export

Goal: balanced levels, then a finished audio file of 2:00–2:30 you can send to anyone.

  • Balance in this order: drums loudest-but-one, melody loudest, bass under the drums, chords behind the melody, sparkle quietest. If you can’t hear the melody clearly, turn the chords down — don’t just turn the melody up.
  • Watch the master meter: peaks should stay out of the red. If it clips, pull the master fader down a little — GarageBand’s export will keep that setting.
  • Make sure the end of song is set just after your last region (drag the end-of-song marker in the ruler) so the export doesn’t include silence — then confirm the total time reads between 2:00 and 2:30.
  • Export WAV (master copy): Share → Export Song to Disk → choose WAV (uncompressed) → Export. This is your best-quality archive.
  • Export MP3 (sharing copy): Share → Export Song to Disk → choose MP3 at the highest quality → Export. Small file, plays everywhere — this is the one you send to friends.
  • Listen to the exported file once on phone speakers and once on earbuds. If the bass disappears on the phone, nudge the bass track up slightly and re-export.
WAV vs MP3: same song, two jobs. WAV = uncompressed original for keeping and future edits; MP3 = compressed copy for sharing. Always export the WAV first.

Melody improvisation — no music knowledge needed

Step 5 told you to improvise — this section shows you how, even if you have never played a note. The Launchkey does the theory for you: with Scale mode on, there are no wrong notes — only choices. Below: five safety rules, three beats to practise over right here in this page, and four worked melody examples you can hear, see and copy.

Five safety rules — tape these to the wall

1

White keys only

In C major or A minor, every white key belongs to the scale, so nothing you play can clash with the loops. The black keys are the only way to go wrong — ignore them completely for your first songs.

2

Scale mode on

On the Launchkey, turn on Scale mode and set it to your song’s key (C major is the friendliest). The pads light up with in-key notes only — the keyboard itself becomes your safety net.

3

Fewer notes, more rhythm

A melody is mostly rhythm. Three notes played with a confident rhythm sound musical; ten notes wandered at random do not. When in doubt: fewer notes, stronger rhythm, more repeats.

4

Start and end at home

Begin phrases on the root note — C in C major, A in A minor, the highlighted key in Step 5’s piano — and land back on it when the phrase ends. Home is what makes a melody sound finished instead of lost.

5

Copy, then wander

Improvisation is not inventing from nothing. Play a short phrase, repeat it exactly, then change just the ending. Question-and-answer phrases are how almost every catchy melody is built.

Hands on the keys — position & fingering

You don’t need piano training — you need a home position and a few finger habits. Set both hands up like the photo: fingers gently curved (as if holding a tennis ball), fingertips on the white keys, wrists level, shoulders dropped. Pianists number the fingers 1 to 5 — thumb is 1, index 2, middle 3, ring 4, pinky 5 — and both hands use the same numbers. Everything below uses them.

Right hand plays the melody

Rest your right hand in home position: thumb (1) on middle C, then 2 on D, 3 on E, 4 on F, 5 on G — one finger parked on each key, as in the diagram below. Most beginner melodies live inside these five notes. If a phrase reaches higher, shift the whole hand up instead of stretching one finger.

Chords are fingers 1 · 3 · 5

A basic chord (a triad) is every other note: for a C chord, press C with 1, E with 3, G with 5 — all three together, one relaxed squeeze. Keep that same hand shape and move it so the thumb lands on F, then on G, and you have the F and G chords too. The Launchkey’s Chord mode (see the Gear section) plays the whole chord from your thumb alone — use it to explore; use the real 1·3·5 shape when you record.

Left hand keeps it simple

Your left hand has the easy job: mirror home position below middle C (pinky 5 on the lower C), and mostly just tap the root note of each chord on beat 1 — finger 5 or 1, one note at a time. GarageBand’s bass loops do the heavy lifting anyway; the left hand is for later songs.

Posture beats strength

Press with the fingertips, not flat fingers; keep the finger joints curved and the wrist floating level — never press down from the shoulder. If your hand aches, you’re tensing up: shake it out, drop your shoulders, go again. The Launchkey’s keys are smaller and touch-sensitive, so accuracy beats force — a firm tap is plenty.

The four chords — C · G · Am · F

Almost every song in this guide — and thousands of pop songs — runs on just these four chords, in this order: the I–V–vi–IV loop from the planner. Each chord is only three notes, all on white keys, all played with the same right-hand shape from above: fingers 1 · 3 · 5. Learn these four cards and you can play the chords under every beat and example below — each card shows the notes written on the staff, the exact keys to press, and a button to hear the chord.

Practise the loop: play C → G → Am → F, one chord per slow count of four, always with fingers 1 · 3 · 5. Keep the hand shape frozen and just slide it so the thumb lands on each new root note — after ten loops your hand will find the chords by itself. That loop is the chord track of your first song.

Practise here: three beats to improvise over

Press play, then play along on your Launchkey (or just listen and tap the rhythms). Each beat loops its four chords forever — the highlighted chip shows where you are. In GarageBand, build the same thing with Steps 2–3 (drums + bass + chords), turn the cycle region on, and jam.

Launchkey 37 [MK3] — the controls you’ll actually use

Thirty-seven keys, sixteen pads and a row of knobs look like a lot. For this workflow you only need six things:

Keys

Your melody lives here. In C major / A minor, white keys only — every note fits the loops.

Scale mode

Lights the pads with in-key notes and keeps your playing inside the scale. Set it to the project key before recording (Step 5).

Chord mode

Hold one key, get a full chord. Handy for testing which chord change feels right under your melody.

Arpeggiator

Hold a chord and the Launchkey plays a rhythmic pattern from its notes. Record its output as instant sparkle-layer material.

Octave + / −

Shifts the whole keyboard up or down an octave — your 37 keys can reach bass notes and high melody notes without moving your hands.

Pads & knobs

Pads send MIDI notes too — tap out a shaker rhythm on them, then quantize it like any melody. In GarageBand the knobs send standard MIDI CC; leave deep mapping for song #2.

When something sounds wrong

Launchkey makes no sound

Check the USB cable is fully in, select the Software Instrument track (not an audio track), and make sure the track’s record-enable / monitoring is on. GarageBand → Settings → Audio/MIDI should list the Launchkey as a MIDI input.

Melody sounds messy

That’s normal before Step 6. Quantize at 1/16 with ~85% strength, tick “Limit to Key”, delete stray notes. Still messy? The motif is too complicated — keep one rhythm, fewer notes.

A loop clashes with everything

Don’t fight it — swap it. Check its key in the Loop Browser and stay in your song’s key (or relative minor), and prefer loops from the same name family as your drums.

Chorus doesn’t feel bigger

Add the extra percussion layer and the sparkle layer only in the chorus, and remove something from the verse. Contrast, not volume, creates the lift.

Song is too long / too short

Go back to the planner: trimming or adding 4 bars at 116 BPM changes the length by about 8 seconds. Adjust bars first, tempo second.

Before you call it finished

  • Total length is between 2:00 and 2:30 — check the planner map against the GarageBand ruler.
  • Every section changes at least one layer — intro, verse and chorus are easy to tell apart with your eyes closed.
  • Melody is quantized but not robotic — 1/16 or 1/8 grid, strength around 75–90%, “Limit to Key” ticked.
  • Master meter never hits the red — pull the master fader down if it does, then re-check.
  • WAV master exported and MP3 sharing copy exported — Share → Export Song to Disk, twice.
  • Project saved with a clear name — “First Song 001” beats “Untitled 7” when you come back next week.

Launchkey 37 [MK3] — the full tour

Everything about your keyboard on one page: what each part does, where every note lives, and how to turn the whole thing into a drum kit. One big idea first — the Launchkey makes no sound of its own. It sends MIDI notes down the USB cable, and GarageBand turns them into sound. Every control below is just a different way of sending those notes.

Top view — the five zones

A simplified top view of the board. Find the five zones and the rest of this page is just detail:

1Keys — 37

Three octaves plus one extra key, running C to C. Every note is mapped in the diagram below.

2Pads — 16

RGB finger pads that change job with their mode — Session, Drum, Keys or User. Drum mode gets its own section at the bottom of this page.

3Knobs — 8

Endless encoders sending MIDI CC. In GarageBand they can drive Smart Controls and plug-in knobs; for your first song you can safely ignore them.

4Pitch & mod wheels

The two wheels at the far left of the keys. Pitch bends notes and springs back; Mod usually adds vibrato and stays where you leave it.

5Screen, buttons & Shift

The little screen names whatever you just pressed. Shift is the master key — hold it to reach the second job printed in grey under many buttons and pads.

Every note on the board

Thirty-seven keys, but only seven note names: C D E F G A B repeat in every octave, and every C is tinted so home is always easy to find. The black keys are the in-between notes, named with a sharp sign — C♯ is the black key immediately to the right of C. And with Octave + / −, these same 37 keys reach far lower and higher than this picture.

Key controls, one by one

Pitch wheel

Push up or pull down to bend the pitch of the note you’re holding, guitar-style. Let go and it springs back to the middle. Beautiful on synth leads; keep it away from piano sounds.

Mod wheel

Stays wherever you leave it. On most sounds it adds vibrato — a gentle wobble — or opens the tone up. Park it halfway while you hold a chord and listen.

Octave + / −

Shifts every key up or down exactly one octave per press; the buttons glow brighter the further you’ve gone. Press both at once to jump back to zero.

Transpose — Shift + Octave

Hold Shift and tap Octave + / − to move by single semitones instead. That’s how you move a song from C to D without relearning any fingering.

Scale mode

Set your key (C major, say) and the pads light up with only the notes that fit. Together with the guide’s white-keys rule, wrong notes become almost impossible.

Chord mode

One key in, a full chord out — built from your chosen key. Use it to test chord changes quickly; Fixed Chord stamps one chord shape under every key.

Arpeggiator

Hold a chord and it plays the notes as a repeating pattern — up, down, or random, at a rate you set. Record the output into GarageBand as ready-made sparkle.

Shift — the grey printing

Nearly every button and pad has a second job printed in grey underneath. Hold Shift to use those jobs: pad modes, transpose and arp settings all live behind Shift.

Turn it into a drum kit

Two ways to play drums on the Launchkey — pads that feel like a drum machine, or the keys themselves. Both start from the same place: a GarageBand track whose sound is a drum kit.

Method A — pads as a drum machine

  1. In GarageBand, add a Software Instrument track and choose a Drum Kit sound from the Library — anything with “Kit” in its name.
  2. On the Launchkey, hold Shift. The top row of pads lights up — it’s now the pad-mode selector.
  3. Tap the pad for Drum mode (the screen names the mode as you pick it), then release Shift.
  4. The 16 pads are now drum pads, one kit piece each, and they’re touch-sensitive: tap harder, hit louder. If the piece you want isn’t on a pad, Octave + / − shifts the pads’ range.
  5. Record like any other track — then quantize your finger-drumming with Step 6 of the guide.

Method B — the keys as a drum kit

  1. Same GarageBand start: a Software Instrument track with a Drum Kit sound. Use a Kit, not a Drummer track — Drummer plays itself; a kit waits for your fingers.
  2. Play any key: instead of a pitch you hear a drum. Every key is a different piece of the kit, in the standard map drawn below.
  3. Use Octave − / + to slide the kit until kick and snare sit under your strongest fingers — the map shows the home octave.
  4. Mind the black keys: hi-hats, clap and stick live there. Crash and ride wait on C♯ and D♯ just above the map’s top C.
Which one should you use? Pads for rhythms you want to feel in your fingertips; keys when you want the whole kit under two hands — or a drum part you can play like a melody and quantize afterwards.
